Building a Lean-certified knowledge base

Vision

Build a mathematically accurate, general and extensible knowledge base, supported by Lean-checked proofs. It should help people understand the subject, use its results in their own work, and develop the theory further.

Requirements

  1. Mathematical statements, proofs and Lean certification. State each definition and theorem precisely, including its parameters, hypotheses and conventions. Give each theorem a complete readable proof and link its formal counterpart to the exact Lean declaration. A Lean-certified result has a completed, checked Lean proof and reviewed agreement between its written and formal statements. Record the checked revision, dependencies and axioms, and identify conditional results, conjectures and work in progress.

  2. Generality. State definitions and results in the greatest generality supported by the arguments. Keep parameters explicit, identify where each assumption enters the proof, and derive important special cases from the general results.

  3. Consistent notation and conventions. Use a consistent set of notation and conventions throughout the exposition and Lean code. Explain how alternative formulations relate, including the equivalences or comparison maps needed to move between them.

  4. Reusable Lean interfaces and extensible type design. Build one coherent library on mathlib's types, structures and theorems, following its notation and API conventions. Choose general data structures and reusable interfaces that keep routine constructions and theorem applications simple. Use structures, typeclasses and explicit parameters according to their roles, keeping instance inference predictable and coercions coherent. Make multiple inheritance paths to the same structure agree definitionally to avoid conflicting instance diamonds. Check the design on representative examples and extensions.

  5. Explanations, examples and applications. Explain what the main definitions capture. Introduce the idea of a proof before its details and work out significant calculations. Include worked examples and applications showing how the results can be used in further arguments.

  6. Linked concepts and layered navigation. Make every mathematical concept in a statement clickable, with symbols and hypotheses linked to the definitions and conventions used there. Let readers move from a short explanation to full definitions, examples and prerequisite material. Provide direct links to proofs and Lean code. Show each page's place in the topic, its connections to other results and a clear route back to the original statement.

  7. Attribution, historical context and references. For important theorems, name the people who proved them and explain their place in the development of the subject, including later generalizations and corrections. Give full references with authors, titles, publication details, page or theorem locations, editions and DOI links. Identify contributors to the exposition and Lean formalization as well.
